# What Are Real-World Assets?

Real-World Assets, commonly called **RWAs**, are physical or traditional financial assets whose ownership, value or economic rights can be represented digitally.

Examples include:

- Real estate
- Gold and other commodities
- Bonds
- Loans
- Company shares
- Invoices
- Agricultural products
- Intellectual property

In Web3, these assets may be connected to blockchain-based tokens.

## 1. Understanding Real-World Assets

A Real-World Asset exists outside the blockchain.

For example:

- A building exists physically
- Gold exists physically
- A bond represents a financial obligation
- An invoice represents money owed by a customer
- A copyright represents ownership rights over creative work

These assets already have value before they are connected to blockchain technology.

---

## 2. What Makes an Asset an RWA?

An asset may be considered an RWA when it has:

- Identifiable ownership
- Measurable value
- Legal or contractual rights
- Evidence or documentation
- The possibility of being transferred, financed or exchanged

The asset does not become valuable simply because a token is created.

The token should be supported by a genuine asset and clear ownership arrangements.

---

## 3. RWAs and Blockchain

Blockchain can be used to create digital tokens connected to real-world assets.

A token may represent:

- Full ownership
- Fractional ownership
- A right to receive income
- A debt obligation
- Access to an asset
- A claim defined by a legal agreement

For example, a building may be divided into many digital tokens, allowing several participants to hold fractional interests.

---

## 4. The Token Is Not Always the Asset

It is important to understand the difference between:

- The real-world asset
- The blockchain token
- The legal rights attached to the token

Owning a token does not automatically mean that the holder legally owns the physical asset.

The project's contracts and legal structure must clearly explain what the token represents.

---

## 5. Common RWA Examples

### Real Estate

Buildings, land and rental properties may be represented through digital tokens.

### Commodities

Gold, silver, oil and agricultural products can be linked to blockchain-based ownership records.

### Debt and Bonds

Loans, government bonds and corporate debt may be issued or represented digitally.

### Invoices

A business may tokenize unpaid invoices to access funding before customers complete payment.

### Intellectual Property

Royalties from music, films, patents or other creative rights may be represented through tokens.

---

## 6. Why Businesses Use RWA Tokenization

RWA tokenization may help businesses:

- Divide expensive assets into smaller units
- Improve record keeping
- Reach a wider group of participants
- Automate certain payments
- Make ownership transfers more efficient
- Increase transparency

However, every project still needs proper legal, financial and technical structures.

---

## Practical Example

Imagine a commercial property valued at $500,000.

The property is placed under a legally recognized ownership structure.

The project creates 50,000 digital tokens.

Each token represents a defined portion of the economic rights connected to the property.

Before participating, a person should verify:

- Who legally owns the building
- What each token represents
- How rental income is distributed
- Whether tokens can be transferred
- What happens if the property is sold
- Which laws apply

---

## Key Risks

RWA projects may face:

- Unclear legal ownership
- False or overstated asset values
- Poor custody
- Limited liquidity
- Regulatory problems
- Counterparty failure
- Inaccurate documentation

Blockchain records cannot correct false information entered into the system.

## Key Takeaways

- RWAs are physical or traditional financial assets connected to blockchain.
- Tokens may represent ownership, income rights, debt or another legal claim.
- A token does not automatically prove legal ownership.
- Genuine assets, documentation and legal structures are essential.
- RWA tokenization can improve access and efficiency, but it also introduces risks.
