# Tokenized Commodities and Precious Metals

Commodities have been traded for centuries and remain an essential part of the global economy. Through blockchain technology, commodities such as gold, silver and agricultural products can now be represented by digital tokens, allowing ownership or defined economic rights to be recorded more efficiently.

Tokenization does not change the physical commodity itself. Instead, it creates a digital representation of rights connected to that commodity according to the project's legal structure.

## 1. What Are Commodities?

Commodities are raw materials or primary goods that are bought and sold in global markets.

Examples include:

- Gold
- Silver
- Platinum
- Palladium
- Copper
- Oil
- Natural gas
- Coffee
- Cocoa
- Wheat
- Corn
- Cotton

Many of these assets have standardized market values, making them suitable candidates for tokenization.

---

## 2. What Is Commodity Tokenization?

Commodity tokenization is the process of creating blockchain tokens that represent ownership or defined rights connected to physical commodities.

Depending on the project's structure, a token may represent:

- Ownership of a quantity of a commodity.
- A claim backed by stored reserves.
- Fractional economic rights.
- Access to future delivery.
- Another legally defined interest.

The exact rights depend on the legal documentation supporting the project.

---

## 3. Why Tokenize Commodities?

Businesses and investors may choose commodity tokenization because it can offer:

### Fractional Ownership

Expensive commodities such as gold can be divided into smaller investment units.

### Easier Transfers

Blockchain may simplify ownership transfers while maintaining transparent records.

### Global Accessibility

Participants from different regions may be able to access the same investment opportunities, subject to applicable laws.

### Improved Record Keeping

Every token transfer is recorded on the blockchain, creating a transparent transaction history.

---

## 4. The Importance of Custody

Unlike digital-only assets, commodities must usually be stored securely.

Examples include:

- Gold stored in professional vaults.
- Silver held by licensed custodians.
- Oil stored in approved facilities.
- Agricultural products stored in certified warehouses.

Professional custody helps protect the underlying asset while supporting confidence in the tokenized project.

---

## 5. Verification and Auditing

Investors need confidence that the commodity actually exists.

Professional projects often provide:

- Independent audits.
- Inventory reports.
- Storage confirmations.
- Insurance documentation.
- Periodic reserve verification.

Regular verification strengthens trust between token holders and project operators.

---

## 6. Challenges

Commodity tokenization also presents challenges.

These include:

- Storage costs.
- Insurance expenses.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Market price volatility.
- Custodian risk.
- Cross-border regulations.
- Verification of reserves.

A blockchain cannot confirm whether physical commodities remain in storage without trusted external verification.

---

## Practical Example

A company owns **500 kilograms of gold** stored in a secure vault.

The company creates **500,000 blockchain tokens**.

Each token represents rights equivalent to a small fraction of the stored gold, as defined by the project's legal agreements.

The blockchain records:

- Token ownership.
- Transfers.
- Transaction history.

The physical gold remains protected by an independent custodian.

Regular audit reports verify that the gold reserve continues to support the tokenized project.

## Key Takeaways

- Commodity tokenization connects physical commodities with blockchain technology.
- Tokens represent clearly defined rights rather than replacing the physical commodity.
- Professional custody and independent verification are essential.
- Blockchain improves transparency but cannot independently verify physical reserves.
- Strong governance builds confidence in commodity-backed tokenization projects.
