# Understanding Trading Psychology

Trading psychology refers to the thoughts, emotions, beliefs and behaviours that influence a trader's decisions.

A trader may have:

- a strong strategy
- accurate market analysis
- proper risk management rules
- a complete trading plan
- reliable trading tools

Yet the trader may still lose money by failing to control behaviour.

Trading performance depends not only on what the market does.

It also depends on how the trader responds to:

- uncertainty
- profit
- loss
- pressure
- boredom
- missed opportunities
- winning streaks
- losing streaks
- market volatility

Understanding trading psychology helps the trader identify emotional and mental risks before they lead to poor decisions.

---

# Why Trading Psychology Matters

Trading decisions are made under uncertainty.

Before entering a trade, the trader cannot know with certainty whether price will move toward the target or the stop-loss.

This uncertainty can produce emotions such as:

- fear
- greed
- hope
- frustration
- excitement
- regret
- anxiety
- overconfidence

These emotions are natural.

The problem begins when emotions control the trading process.

A professional trader does not need to eliminate emotions completely.

The trader must learn to recognize emotions and prevent them from overriding the trading plan.

---

# Trading Is a Decision-Making Activity

Trading is not only chart analysis.

It is a repeated decision-making process.

The trader must decide:

- whether to trade
- whether to wait
- what market to trade
- how much to risk
- where to enter
- where to place the stop-loss
- where to take profit
- when to stop trading
- whether a setup is still valid
- whether emotions are affecting judgement

The quality of these decisions determines long-term performance.

---

# Knowledge Does Not Automatically Produce Discipline

A trader may know that:

- every trade needs a stop-loss
- position size must be calculated
- total session risk must remain controlled
- revenge trading is dangerous
- chasing price is a mistake
- overtrading increases risk

However, knowing the rule does not guarantee that the trader will follow it.

Psychology becomes important when the trader must apply the rule under pressure.

---

# The Conflict Between Logic and Emotion

Logical trading may require the trader to:

- accept a small loss
- remain outside the market
- wait several hours for confirmation
- stop after reaching the session loss limit
- avoid increasing position size
- allow a winning trade to reach its planned target

Emotion may encourage the trader to:

- hold a losing trade
- enter too early
- chase a missed move
- close profit too quickly
- increase risk after a loss
- take unnecessary trades

The trading plan represents logic.

Impulsive behaviour often represents emotion.

---

# The Role of Uncertainty

No strategy wins every trade.

Even a valid setup may fail because:

- market conditions change
- unexpected news appears
- liquidity changes
- the setup was statistically valid but unsuccessful
- price reacts differently than expected

A trader must become comfortable with uncertainty.

The objective is not to predict every market movement.

The objective is to make controlled decisions with positive long-term expectancy.

---

# Probability-Based Thinking

Professional trading is based on probabilities rather than certainty.

A trader should think:

"This setup meets my rules and has a reasonable probability of success."

The trader should not think:

"This trade must win."

Probability-based thinking accepts that:

- any individual trade may lose
- a winning strategy may experience losing streaks
- one loss does not prove that the strategy is useless
- one win does not prove that the strategy is perfect
- performance must be judged over many trades

---

# Outcome vs Process

The outcome is the financial result of a trade.

The process is how the trade was selected, executed and managed.

A trade may produce:

- a good process and a loss
- a good process and a profit
- a bad process and a loss
- a bad process and a profit

A profitable trade is not automatically a good trade.

A losing trade is not automatically a bad trade.

---

# Good Process, Losing Result

A good loss may occur when:

- the setup matched the strategy
- confirmation appeared
- risk was calculated
- position size was correct
- the stop-loss was respected
- session risk remained controlled
- the market moved against the trade

This is a normal business expense of trading.

---

# Bad Process, Winning Result

A bad win may occur when the trader:

- entered without confirmation
- used excessive leverage
- ignored the stop-loss
- chased price
- risked too much
- acted emotionally

The trade may still make profit because of luck.

Rewarding this behaviour can lead to larger future losses.

---

# Psychological Capital

Financial capital is the money used for trading.

Psychological capital is the trader's ability to remain:

- calm
- focused
- patient
- disciplined
- confident
- realistic

Psychological capital can be reduced by:

- repeated losses
- excessive risk
- lack of sleep
- financial pressure
- constant screen watching
- emotional conflict
- trading without breaks

A trader should protect psychological capital just as carefully as financial capital.

---

# Emotional Awareness

Emotional awareness means recognizing what the trader is feeling before and during trading.

Questions may include:

- Am I afraid of losing?
- Am I excited because of recent profits?
- Am I angry after a stopped-out trade?
- Am I trying to recover money quickly?
- Am I bored?
- Am I trading because others are making profit?
- Am I emotionally attached to this position?
- Am I afraid to miss the move?

Awareness creates an opportunity to pause before acting.

---

# Emotional Control vs Emotional Suppression

Emotional control does not mean pretending that emotions do not exist.

Suppressing emotions may cause them to influence decisions without the trader recognizing them.

Emotional control means:

1. Recognize the emotion.
2. Identify what caused it.
3. Avoid immediate reaction.
4. return to the trading plan.
5. Act only if the strategy rules remain valid.

The objective is controlled action, not emotional denial.

---

# Common Emotional States in Trading

Trading emotions may include:

- fear of losing
- fear of missing out
- greed
- hope
- frustration
- anger
- regret
- excitement
- boredom
- overconfidence
- doubt
- anxiety

Each emotion can influence behaviour differently.

---

# Fear

Fear may cause the trader to:

- avoid valid setups
- close winning trades too early
- reduce position size after the trade begins
- move the stop-loss too close
- hesitate during execution
- avoid trading after a normal loss

Fear often increases when the trader risks too much.

Proper position sizing can reduce emotional pressure.

---

# Greed

Greed may cause the trader to:

- increase position size without justification
- remove the take-profit
- hold beyond the trading plan
- open too many positions
- use excessive leverage
- continue trading after reaching the session goal
- ignore warning signs

Greed often appears after profits or during strong market movement.

---

# Hope

Hope becomes dangerous when it replaces analysis.

A trader may keep a losing position open while hoping that price will return.

This may lead to:

- widening the stop-loss
- removing the stop-loss
- adding to a losing trade
- ignoring invalidation
- allowing a small loss to become large

A trade should remain open because the strategy remains valid, not because the trader hopes for recovery.

---

# Regret

Regret may occur after:

- missing a profitable trade
- closing too early
- taking a loss
- breaking a rule
- choosing the wrong market
- failing to enter

Regret can lead to chasing price or making immediate emotional trades.

A missed opportunity should be recorded and reviewed.

It should not be followed by impulsive action.

---

# Frustration

Frustration may develop when:

- several trades lose
- the market remains slow
- price nearly reaches the target and reverses
- a valid entry is missed
- the platform executes poorly

Frustration can cause overtrading and revenge trading.

The trader should step away when frustration begins affecting judgement.

---

# Excitement

Excitement may appear after:

- a large profit
- a strong winning streak
- a fast market move
- public praise
- discovering a new strategy

Excessive excitement can reduce caution.

The trader may begin to believe that normal risk rules are no longer necessary.

---

# Boredom

Boredom may cause the trader to:

- enter low-quality setups
- trade in the middle of a range
- switch markets repeatedly
- use lower timeframes for entertainment
- take random trades

Trading should not be used as a source of constant excitement.

No trade is a valid outcome.

---

# Anxiety

Anxiety may cause:

- constant chart checking
- inability to hold a trade
- repeated stop movement
- poor sleep
- premature exits
- hesitation

Anxiety may indicate:

- excessive position size
- unclear rules
- financial pressure
- overexposure
- lack of confidence in the strategy

The solution should focus on the cause, not only the feeling.

---

# Overconfidence

Overconfidence may develop after several winning trades.

It may cause the trader to:

- increase risk
- ignore confirmation
- take unfamiliar setups
- believe losses cannot happen
- use excessive leverage
- stop journaling
- abandon the trading plan

Winning streaks should not change risk rules without tested evidence.

---

# Lack of Confidence

Lack of confidence may cause:

- hesitation
- missed entries
- constant strategy changes
- reliance on other traders
- early exits
- reduced discipline

Confidence should come from:

- education
- practice
- backtesting
- forward testing
- journaling
- repeated rule-following

Confidence should not come from hope or one successful trade.

---

# Emotional Triggers

An emotional trigger is an event that creates a strong reaction.

Possible triggers include:

- a large loss
- several losses in a row
- a missed opportunity
- watching others make profit
- unexpected market movement
- broker execution problems
- public criticism
- financial pressure
- personal stress

The trader should identify personal triggers through journaling.

---

# Beliefs About Money

A trader's beliefs about money can influence behaviour.

Possible beliefs include:

- money must be recovered immediately
- every trading day must be profitable
- a small account must grow quickly
- losing means failure
- taking more risk creates success
- professional traders never lose

These beliefs are unrealistic and dangerous.

Trading requires acceptance of controlled losses.

---

# The Need to Be Right

Some traders become emotionally attached to being correct.

They may:

- refuse to close invalid trades
- argue with market movement
- add to losing positions
- ignore contrary evidence
- blame the broker or market
- avoid admitting mistakes

The market does not reward personal pride.

The trader's objective is not to prove intelligence.

The objective is to manage risk and follow the plan.

---

# Ego in Trading

Ego may cause the trader to:

- believe rules do not apply
- reject feedback
- trade larger to impress others
- hide losses
- exaggerate profits
- refuse to stop
- compete with other traders

Trading should not become a competition for attention.

Account protection is more important than appearance.

---

# Attachment to a Market Opinion

A trader may become attached to a bullish or bearish opinion.

This may cause the trader to ignore:

- structure changes
- failed breakouts
- invalidation
- opposing momentum
- important news

A market opinion should remain flexible.

The trader should respond to evidence rather than defend a prediction.

---

# Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias occurs when the trader searches only for information that supports an existing opinion.

Example:

The trader expects price to rise and ignores all bearish evidence.

The trader may:

- focus only on bullish indicators
- dismiss resistance
- ignore higher-timeframe weakness
- follow only analysts who agree

A complete analysis should examine both supporting and opposing evidence.

---

# Recency Bias

Recency bias occurs when recent events are given too much importance.

Examples include:

- believing a strategy is perfect after three wins
- believing a strategy is useless after three losses
- expecting the next trade to copy the previous one
- increasing risk after recent success

Performance should be judged over a meaningful sample.

---

# Loss Aversion

Loss aversion means losses may feel more painful than equivalent profits feel rewarding.

This may cause traders to:

- hold losing trades too long
- close winning trades too early
- avoid taking valid setups
- move stops farther away
- refuse to accept small losses

A predefined stop-loss reduces emotional negotiation.

---

# Sunk Cost Fallacy

The sunk cost fallacy occurs when a trader continues supporting a bad decision because time or money has already been committed.

Example:

"I have already lost so much in this trade, so I must keep holding."

Past losses cannot justify future risk.

The decision should be based on the current validity of the setup.

---

# Gambler's Fallacy

The gambler's fallacy is the belief that a result is due simply because the opposite result occurred repeatedly.

Example:

"I have lost four trades, so the next one must win."

Each trade should be judged on its own valid setup.

A losing streak does not guarantee an immediate winning trade.

---

# The Hot-Hand Belief

The hot-hand belief is the assumption that recent success guarantees continued success.

Example:

"I won five trades, so I should double my risk."

Winning streaks can end at any time.

Risk rules should remain stable.

---

# Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when the trader becomes fixed on one price or expectation.

Examples include:

- refusing to sell below the original entry
- expecting price to return to a previous high
- believing an analyst's target must be reached
- treating one support level as permanent

Market structure can change.

The trader must update decisions when evidence changes.

---

# Overtrading

Overtrading means taking more trades than the strategy or trading plan allows.

Possible causes include:

- boredom
- greed
- revenge
- excitement
- financial pressure
- fear of missing out

Overtrading may increase:

- transaction costs
- mistakes
- emotional stress
- correlated exposure
- drawdown

The trading plan should define the maximum number of trades.

---

# Undertrading

Undertrading means avoiding valid trades because of fear, doubt or hesitation.

Possible causes include:

- recent losses
- lack of confidence
- oversized risk
- unclear rules
- excessive analysis

The solution is not to force trades.

The trader should improve clarity, practise execution and reduce risk if necessary.

---

# Impulsive Trading

Impulsive trading occurs when a position is opened without completing the normal decision process.

Examples include:

- entering because of a large candle
- copying another trader immediately
- trading after social media excitement
- buying because price is rising quickly
- selling because price is falling quickly

A pre-trade checklist creates a pause between emotion and action.

---

# Trading Under Financial Pressure

Trading becomes psychologically dangerous when the trader urgently needs profit for:

- rent
- debt
- school fees
- medical expenses
- food
- family responsibilities

Urgency may cause:

- excessive risk
- forced trades
- holding losses
- abandoning the plan

Trading capital should be separated from essential funds.

---

# Trading Under Personal Stress

Stress outside trading can affect market decisions.

Possible sources include:

- family conflict
- poor sleep
- illness
- work pressure
- financial problems
- emotional distress

The trader should assess mental readiness before each session.

Taking a break can be a professional decision.

---

# Physical Condition and Trading

Decision-making may be affected by:

- fatigue
- hunger
- dehydration
- poor sleep
- illness
- excessive screen time

A trader should not ignore physical condition.

Trading requires concentration and emotional regulation.

---

# The Psychological Effect of Position Size

Position size strongly affects emotions.

When the risk is too large, the trader may:

- watch every price movement
- close early
- move the stop-loss
- panic
- lose sleep
- become emotionally attached

A position may be mathematically possible but psychologically unsuitable.

Risk should remain small enough for the trader to follow the plan calmly.

---

# Risk Per Trade and Emotional Stability

Lower risk per trade can help the trader:

- accept losses
- remain patient
- avoid panic
- follow the stop-loss
- think in probabilities
- survive losing streaks

The goal is not to create excitement.

The goal is to maintain consistent decision-making.

---

# Maximum Session Risk

For this course:

**Total risk during one trading session must never exceed 5% of total trading capital.**

This includes:

- all open positions
- pending orders
- correlated trades
- additional entries

The 5% level is a maximum ceiling.

Using less risk may provide stronger psychological stability.

---

# Risk-to-Reward and Psychology

For this course, traders should generally seek an average risk-to-reward ratio of approximately:

**1:3**

A structured risk-to-reward plan may help reduce emotional decisions.

However, traders may still interfere by:

- closing profit too early
- moving the stop farther away
- removing the target
- taking profit randomly

Risk-to-reward only works when the plan is followed.

---

# Accepting Losses

Losses are part of trading.

Acceptance does not mean enjoying losses.

It means understanding that a controlled loss is possible even when the process is correct.

The trader should distinguish between:

- normal strategy loss
- execution error
- emotional mistake
- excessive risk

Each type requires a different response.

---

# Controlled Losses

A controlled loss should:

- remain within the risk limit
- occur at the predefined stop-loss
- not threaten the account
- not cause emotional escalation
- be recorded in the journal

One controlled loss should not determine the next trade.

---

# Fear of Loss

Fear of loss may increase when:

- the position is too large
- the trader uses essential money
- the strategy is untested
- the stop-loss is unclear
- the trader expects every trade to win

The solution may include:

- reducing risk
- improving testing
- using a clear stop
- accepting probabilities
- separating essential funds

---

# Winning Streak Psychology

A winning streak can create:

- excitement
- overconfidence
- larger risk
- weaker setup selection
- excessive trading

The trader should maintain the same rules after wins.

A winning streak does not remove market uncertainty.

---

# Losing Streak Psychology

A losing streak may create:

- fear
- frustration
- self-doubt
- anger
- revenge trading
- strategy switching

The trader should:

- reduce risk if required
- review whether rules were followed
- check market conditions
- pause if emotional
- avoid immediate strategy changes

A losing streak may be statistically normal.

---

# Emotional Neutrality

Emotional neutrality means avoiding extreme reactions to individual trades.

The trader should not become:

- excessively excited after profit
- emotionally destroyed after loss
- arrogant after success
- desperate after failure

The focus should remain on the full trading process.

---

# Detachment From Individual Trades

The trader should not attach identity or self-worth to one trade.

A trade is one event within a larger sample.

The trader can say:

- this setup followed my rules
- this setup failed
- the risk was controlled
- I will record and review it

This approach reduces emotional pressure.

---

# The Importance of Routine

A trading routine creates structure.

A routine may include:

1. Check physical and emotional condition.
2. Review economic news.
3. Mark important levels.
4. Identify market conditions.
5. Confirm session risk.
6. Wait for approved setups.
7. Use the pre-trade checklist.
8. Record each trade.
9. Stop at the defined limit.
10. Review the session.

Routine reduces random decision-making.

---

# Pre-Session Psychological Check

Before trading, ask:

1. Did I sleep adequately?
2. Am I calm?
3. Am I under financial pressure?
4. Am I trying to recover a previous loss?
5. Am I excited after recent profits?
6. Am I distracted?
7. Can I accept the planned risk?
8. Will I respect the session limit?
9. Am I prepared to have no trade today?

If the trader is not mentally prepared, the session may be postponed.

---

# Pre-Trade Psychological Check

Before entering, ask:

1. Is this trade part of my plan?
2. Am I entering because of analysis or emotion?
3. Am I afraid of missing out?
4. Am I trying to recover money?
5. Is the position size comfortable?
6. Can I accept the stop-loss?
7. Am I willing to let the trade follow the plan?
8. Is total session risk below 5%?
9. Does the setup support realistic risk-to-reward?
10. What would cancel the trade?

---

# During-Trade Psychological Check

While the trade is open, ask:

- Has the setup actually changed?
- Am I reacting to normal price movement?
- Am I watching the chart excessively?
- Am I tempted to widen the stop?
- Am I closing early because of fear?
- Am I holding beyond the target because of greed?
- Am I following the management rules?

The trade should be managed according to the plan, not emotion.

---

# Post-Trade Psychological Review

After the trade, record:

- emotional state before entry
- emotional state during the trade
- emotional state after exit
- whether rules were followed
- whether position size affected behaviour
- whether the result influenced the next decision
- whether a break is needed

This builds emotional awareness over time.

---

# Trading Journal for Psychology

A psychological trading journal may include:

- setup
- risk percentage
- confidence level
- emotional state
- reason for entry
- fear level
- greed level
- whether the plan was followed
- result
- lesson learned

Patterns may become visible after several trades.

---

# Emotional Rating Scale

A trader may rate emotions from 1 to 5.

Example:

| Emotional State | Rating |
|---|---:|
| Calm | 1 |
| Slightly uncomfortable | 2 |
| Distracted | 3 |
| Highly emotional | 4 |
| Unable to follow rules | 5 |

The plan may prohibit trading at levels 4 or 5.

---

# Identifying Personal Patterns

Journal review may reveal patterns such as:

- losses increase after poor sleep
- overtrading occurs after the first loss
- risk increases after three wins
- missed trades lead to chasing
- boredom appears during slow sessions
- large positions lead to early exits

Once identified, these patterns can be managed with specific rules.

---

# Creating Psychological Rules

Examples of psychological rules include:

- stop trading after two emotional mistakes
- take a break after every loss
- do not increase risk after a win
- do not chase missed entries
- do not trade while angry
- close the platform after reaching the session limit
- record "No Trade" when no setup appears

Rules should respond to the trader's personal weaknesses.

---

# The Pause Technique

The pause technique creates time between emotion and action.

When an emotional urge appears:

1. Remove the hand from the trading controls.
2. Take several slow breaths.
3. Identify the emotion.
4. Read the relevant trading rule.
5. Recalculate risk.
6. Enter only if the setup remains valid.

A short pause may prevent a large mistake.

---

# Breathing and Attention

Controlled breathing may help reduce immediate stress.

The trader may:

- breathe slowly
- move away from the screen briefly
- avoid watching every tick
- return only after calming down

This is not a trading strategy.

It is a method for restoring decision quality.

---

# Taking a Break

A trader should consider taking a break when:

- session risk limit is reached
- several losses occur
- anger appears
- rules are being broken
- concentration declines
- physical exhaustion develops
- personal stress becomes severe

Stopping is part of professional risk management.

---

# Environment and Psychology

The trading environment can affect behaviour.

Possible distractions include:

- social media
- messaging applications
- noisy surroundings
- multiple trading groups
- constant signal notifications
- watching too many analysts

A focused environment can improve discipline.

---

# Social Media Influence

Social media may create:

- fear of missing out
- unrealistic profit expectations
- comparison
- impulsive entries
- strategy switching

A trader should not enter because another person posted a winning trade.

Every setup must pass the trader's own rules.

---

# Comparing Results With Others

Comparison can cause:

- impatience
- excessive risk
- discouragement
- copying
- hidden losses
- pressure to perform

Different traders may have different:

- capital
- experience
- strategies
- risk tolerance
- market access
- time availability

The trader should compare current performance with personal rules and previous performance.

---

# Signals and Psychological Dependence

Depending completely on signals may prevent the trader from understanding:

- why the trade exists
- where invalidation occurs
- how risk should be calculated
- whether market conditions have changed

A trader should not follow a signal blindly.

Personal analysis and risk control remain necessary.

---

# The Illusion of Control

The trader controls:

- entry decision
- position size
- stop-loss
- target
- trading hours
- number of trades
- behaviour

The trader does not control:

- market direction
- news surprises
- liquidity changes
- other traders
- every execution price
- whether one trade wins

Psychological stability improves when the trader focuses on controllable factors.

---

# Responsibility

A professional trader accepts responsibility for:

- risk taken
- rules followed
- mistakes made
- strategy selected
- review process

Responsibility does not mean blaming oneself for every market loss.

It means taking ownership of controllable decisions.

---

# Building Self-Trust

Self-trust develops when the trader repeatedly follows personal rules.

It is damaged when the trader:

- promises to stop and continues
- ignores risk limits
- changes plans during trades
- hides mistakes
- trades emotionally

Self-trust can be rebuilt through small consistent actions.

---

# Confidence From Evidence

Strong trading confidence should come from:

- documented strategy rules
- backtesting
- forward testing
- controlled risk
- journaling
- repeated execution
- performance review

Confidence based only on excitement is fragile.

---

# Discipline Over Motivation

Motivation changes from day to day.

Discipline means following the plan even when:

- the market is slow
- the trader feels bored
- a previous trade lost
- a previous trade won
- others are making money
- the next setup takes time

A professional process should not depend entirely on temporary motivation.

---

# Realistic Expectations

Unrealistic expectations may include:

- every day must be profitable
- the account must double quickly
- every setup must win
- losses must be recovered immediately
- full-time income must begin immediately

Realistic expectations support patience and controlled risk.

Trading skill develops over time.

---

# Trading as a Business Process

A professional trading mindset treats trading like a business.

A business expects:

- operating costs
- uncertainty
- periods of profit
- periods of loss
- record keeping
- review
- risk control
- continuous improvement

A controlled losing trade may be treated as a normal operating cost.

---

# Psychological Warning Signs

The trader should stop and reassess when:

- position size increases emotionally
- stop-losses are removed
- losses are hidden
- sleep is affected
- essential money is used
- trading becomes compulsive
- anger controls decisions
- the session limit is ignored
- every market move feels urgent
- the trader cannot leave the screen

These warning signs indicate that behaviour is becoming unsafe.

---

# Professional Response to Emotional Pressure

When emotional pressure appears:

1. Stop entering new trades.
2. Review open exposure.
3. Protect existing positions according to the plan.
4. Record the emotional trigger.
5. Leave the platform if necessary.
6. Resume only when calm and within the rules.

The objective is to protect capital and decision quality.

---

# Example Psychological Trading Rule Set

A trader may use rules such as:

- maximum 1% risk per trade
- maximum 5% total session risk
- stop after three losses
- no trade after two rule violations
- no increase in risk after wins
- ten-minute break after a stopped-out trade
- no chasing missed entries
- no trading while angry or exhausted
- journal every trade
- record no-trade sessions

These are examples and should be adapted responsibly.

---

# Psychological Checklist

Before trading:

1. Am I calm?
2. Can I accept a loss?
3. Am I using non-essential capital?
4. Is my position size appropriate?
5. Am I trying to recover money?
6. Am I influenced by social media?
7. Is this setup part of my strategy?
8. Is total session risk controlled?
9. Can I stop when the limit is reached?
10. Am I prepared not to trade?

- risking too much
- trading under financial pressure
- chasing missed opportunities
- holding losses because of hope
- closing profit early because of fear
- increasing risk after wins

# Key Principle

A trader cannot control the outcome of one trade.

The trader can control:

- risk
- preparation
- execution
- discipline
- reaction
- review

Long-term improvement begins by focusing on these controllable factors.

---

# Lesson Summary

Trading psychology refers to the thoughts, emotions, beliefs and behaviours that influence trading decisions.

Important psychological risks include:

- fear
- greed
- hope
- frustration
- regret
- anxiety
- boredom
- overconfidence
- loss aversion
- confirmation bias
- recency bias
- impulsive trading
- overtrading

Professional traders think in probabilities.

They judge performance by process, not only by individual results.

A valid trade can lose.

A poorly executed trade can win through luck.

Psychological discipline requires:

- emotional awareness
- realistic expectations
- appropriate position sizing
- controlled risk
- a written trading plan
- a pre-trade checklist
- journaling
- regular review
- willingness to stop when emotional

For this course, traders should generally aim for an average risk-to-reward ratio of approximately 1:3 when market structure realistically supports it.

Total risk during one trading session must never exceed 5% of total trading capital.

Strong trading psychology does not remove emotions.

It helps the trader recognize emotions and continue making disciplined, rule-based decisions.
