# Executing the Trading Plan

Preparation creates the plan.

Execution turns the plan into action.

Many traders understand what they should do but fail when the market begins moving.

They may:

- enter too early
- chase price
- ignore confirmation
- increase risk
- move stop-losses
- close trades emotionally

Professional execution means following the plan exactly, even when emotions become strong.

---

# What Does Execution Mean?

Execution is the process of applying the trading plan in real market conditions.

It includes:

- waiting for the setup
- confirming the entry
- calculating position size
- placing the stop-loss
- placing the target
- managing the trade
- exiting according to the plan

Execution should be systematic rather than emotional.

---

# Preparation and Execution Are Different

Preparation answers:

- What markets will I trade?
- What levels are important?
- What scenarios are possible?
- How much will I risk?

Execution answers:

- Has the setup actually appeared?
- Has confirmation occurred?
- Does the trade meet all rules?
- Should I enter, wait or avoid the trade?

A good plan still requires disciplined execution.

---

# Follow the Trading Checklist

Before every entry, complete the checklist.

Ask:

1. Is the market on my watchlist?
2. Does the setup match my system?
3. Is the higher-timeframe direction clear?
4. Has confirmation appeared?
5. Is the stop-loss technically valid?
6. Is the target realistic?
7. Does the trade support approximately 1:3 risk-to-reward?
8. Is the position size correct?
9. Will total session risk remain below 5%?
10. Am I emotionally stable?

If important conditions are missing, do not enter.

---

# Wait for the Setup

Patience is one of the most important execution skills.

A trader should not enter because:

- price is moving quickly
- other traders are entering
- the market looks exciting
- a previous opportunity was missed
- there is pressure to make money

The trader should enter only when the defined setup appears.

---

# Recognise a Valid Setup

A valid setup should match written rules.

Depending on the trading system, it may include:

- trend continuation
- pullback
- breakout
- reversal
- support reaction
- resistance rejection

A setup should not be considered valid simply because price is moving.

It must match the trader's system.

---

# Wait for Confirmation

Confirmation helps reduce impulsive entries.

Possible confirmation may include:

- candlestick confirmation
- market structure confirmation
- breakout and retest
- support or resistance reaction
- momentum confirmation
- volume confirmation

The exact confirmation should already be defined in the trading plan.

---

# Avoid Early Entries

Entering before confirmation may lead to:

- false breakouts
- premature reversals
- unnecessary losses
- poor stop-loss placement
- emotional trade management

It is better to miss a trade than to enter a poor-quality setup.

---

# Avoid Chasing Price

Chasing occurs when a trader enters after price has already moved significantly.

This may create:

- poor entry price
- wide stop-loss
- weak risk-to-reward
- emotional pressure
- higher probability of entering near exhaustion

If the planned entry is missed, wait for another opportunity.

---

# Do Not Trade from Fear of Missing Out

Fear of missing out can make traders abandon their rules.

Signs of FOMO include:

- entering without confirmation
- increasing position size
- entering late
- copying another person's trade
- ignoring risk-to-reward

Professional traders accept that opportunities will continue to appear.

---

# Confirm the Entry Price

Before entering, identify the exact entry condition.

Examples include:

- candle closure
- breakout confirmation
- retest of a level
- rejection from support
- rejection from resistance

Avoid vague entries such as:

"Price looks ready."

The entry should have a clear reason.

---

# Calculate Position Size

Position size should be calculated before the trade is opened.

It depends on:

- account balance
- percentage risk
- stop-loss distance
- market value per point or pip

Never choose position size based on emotion.

---

# Respect the Maximum Session Risk

Before entering a new trade, calculate the total risk already active or taken during the session.

For this course:

**Total risk during one trading session must never exceed 5% of total trading capital.**

If the session limit has been reached, no additional trade should be taken.

---

# Confirm Risk-to-Reward

Before entry, compare the planned risk with the potential reward.

For this course, traders should generally seek an average risk-to-reward ratio of approximately:

**1:3**

This means risking one unit to pursue approximately three units of potential reward when market conditions support it.

Do not force a target merely to create a 1:3 ratio.

The target must remain technically realistic.

---

# Place the Stop-Loss Immediately

A stop-loss should be planned before entry and placed as part of the trade.

The stop-loss may be based on:

- technical invalidation
- market structure
- swing high
- swing low
- support
- resistance
- volatility

Trading without a stop-loss exposes capital to uncontrolled risk.

---

# Never Widen the Stop-Loss Emotionally

After entering, price may move against the trade.

A trader may feel tempted to move the stop-loss farther away.

This increases the original risk.

If the setup becomes invalid, accept the planned loss.

Do not change the risk because of hope.

---

# Place the Take-Profit

The target should also be defined before entry.

Possible targets include:

- previous high
- previous low
- support level
- resistance level
- measured target
- fixed risk-to-reward target

The target should match both the strategy and current market conditions.

---

# Do Not Move the Target from Greed

When price approaches the target, greed may encourage the trader to demand more.

Changing the target without a technical reason may turn a winning trade into a smaller profit or a loss.

Follow the trade-management rules already defined.

---

# Manage the Trade According to the Plan

Possible trade-management rules include:

- hold until stop-loss or target
- move stop-loss after specific confirmation
- take partial profit
- use a trailing stop
- exit if the setup becomes invalid

Management rules should be written before entry.

---

# Avoid Constantly Watching Every Price Movement

Watching every small movement may increase:

- anxiety
- fear
- impatience
- emotional exits
- unnecessary adjustments

After entering, allow the trade room to develop according to the plan.

---

# Use Alerts Where Possible

Price alerts can help reduce emotional chart watching.

Alerts may be set for:

- entry zones
- support
- resistance
- stop-loss proximity
- target proximity

Alerts help the trader remain focused without staring constantly at the screen.

---

# Do Not Add to Losing Trades Without a Rule

Adding to a losing position can increase risk quickly.

It should never be done because:

- the trader wants a better average price
- the trader refuses to accept the loss
- the trader believes price must reverse

Only add to positions when the trading system clearly permits it and total risk remains controlled.

---

# Avoid Revenge Trading

After a loss, the trader may feel pressure to recover immediately.

This may lead to:

- larger position sizes
- lower-quality setups
- impulsive entries
- ignoring risk limits
- repeated losses

After a loss, pause and review.

The next trade should be taken only if it fully meets the trading plan.

---

# Handling a Winning Trade

A winning trade can also create emotional problems.

The trader may:

- become overconfident
- increase risk
- enter unnecessary trades
- ignore the checklist
- believe every next trade will win

Treat every trade independently.

One win does not justify breaking the rules.

---

# Handling a Losing Trade

A planned loss is part of trading.

After a losing trade:

1. Accept the result.
2. Record the trade.
3. Confirm whether the rules were followed.
4. Check the remaining session risk.
5. Wait for the next valid setup.

Do not immediately attempt to recover the loss.

---

# Know When Not to Enter

Do not trade when:

- the setup is incomplete
- confirmation is absent
- risk-to-reward is poor
- major news is approaching
- market structure is unclear
- session risk has been reached
- emotions are unstable
- the planned entry has already been missed

Not trading is part of professional execution.

---

# Avoid Overtrading

Overtrading means taking too many trades.

Possible causes include:

- boredom
- greed
- frustration
- excitement
- desire to recover losses
- lack of clear rules

A trading system should define the maximum number of trades allowed during a session.

---

# Stay Focused on Process

During execution, focus on:

- following the checklist
- respecting risk
- waiting for confirmation
- managing according to rules
- documenting decisions

Do not focus only on immediate profit.

A strong process produces better long-term results.

---

# Separate Analysis from Emotion

Market analysis should be based on:

- price structure
- levels
- trend
- confirmation
- trading rules

It should not be based on:

- hope
- fear
- excitement
- social media opinions
- pressure to make money

Objective analysis improves execution.

---

# Avoid Changing the Plan Mid-Trade

Once the trade begins, changes should only occur according to predefined rules.

Do not change:

- stop-loss
- target
- position size
- exit plan

because of emotion.

A plan that changes under pressure is not being followed.

---

# Use One Decision at a Time

Professional execution can be simplified into three possible decisions:

- Enter
- Wait
- Avoid

This reduces confusion.

If the conditions are not complete, the correct decision is usually to wait or avoid.

---

# Example of Professional Execution

A trader identifies an uptrend on the 4-hour chart.

Price returns to support.

The trader waits.

A bullish confirmation candle forms on the entry timeframe.

Before entering, the trader confirms:

- setup matches the system
- stop-loss is below the invalidation level
- target is technically realistic
- risk-to-reward is approximately 1:3
- session risk remains below 5%
- no major news is approaching

The trader enters and manages the position according to the written plan.

This is structured execution.

---

# Example of Poor Execution

A trader sees price rising quickly.

The trader fears missing the move.

Without confirmation, the trader enters late.

The stop-loss is placed randomly.

The risk-to-reward is poor.

When price moves against the trade, the stop-loss is widened.

This is emotional execution.

---

# Execution Scorecard

After each trade, rate the execution.

Possible questions:

- Did I follow the checklist?
- Did I wait for confirmation?
- Was the entry valid?
- Was position size correct?
- Did I respect the stop-loss?
- Did I follow the exit plan?
- Did I remain emotionally controlled?

A trade can receive a high execution score even if it loses.

---

# Common Beginner Mistakes

Avoid:

- entering before confirmation
- chasing price
- trading from FOMO
- using random position sizes
- entering without a stop-loss
- widening stop-losses
- moving targets from greed
- revenge trading
- overtrading
- changing the plan during the trade

These mistakes are usually execution problems rather than analysis problems.

---

# Key Principle

A trading plan has value only when it is followed.

Professional execution means doing what the plan requires, even when:

- the market moves quickly
- a trade is missed
- a loss occurs
- emotions become strong
- another trader has a different opinion

Discipline connects knowledge to results.

---

# Lesson Summary

Executing a trading plan requires the trader to:

- follow the checklist
- wait for a valid setup
- require confirmation
- avoid early entries and price chasing
- calculate position size
- place the stop-loss and target
- respect the maximum session risk
- manage trades according to written rules
- avoid revenge trading and overtrading
- know when not to trade

For this course:

- traders should generally seek an average risk-to-reward ratio of approximately **1:3**
- total trading session risk must **never exceed 5%** of total trading capital

Professional execution is not about reacting quickly.

It is about responding correctly according to the trading plan.
