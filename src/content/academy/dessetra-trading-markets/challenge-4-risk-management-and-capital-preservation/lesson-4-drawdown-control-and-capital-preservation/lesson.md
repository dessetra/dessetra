# Trading Plan & Journal

A trader should not decide everything while price is moving.

Before entering the market, you should already know:

- what you are looking for
- what conditions permit an entry
- how much you can risk
- where the trade becomes invalid
- how the trade will be managed
- when you must stop trading

This is the purpose of a **trading plan**.

After the trade, you also need a record of what actually happened.

That is the purpose of a **trading journal**.

Together:

**Trading Plan = What I should do**

**Trading Journal = What I actually did**

---

## What Is a Trading Plan?

A trading plan is a written set of rules that guides your decisions before, during and after a trading session.

It reduces the number of decisions you must make under pressure.

A useful plan should answer:

1. What markets will I trade?
2. When will I trade?
3. What setup am I looking for?
4. What confirmation is required?
5. How much will I risk?
6. Where will I place my Stop Loss?
7. What R:R will I consider?
8. When will I reject a trade?
9. How will I manage an open trade?
10. When must I stop trading?

If these questions have no clear answers, emotions can easily fill the gap.

---

## Keep the Plan Simple

A trading plan does not need to be a complicated document.

The rules should be clear enough that you can answer:

**Does this setup qualify â€” Yes or No?**

Avoid rules that change every time the market moves.

For example:

Weak rule:

> "Buy when the chart looks bullish."

Better rule:

> "Consider a Buy only when the required trend, structure, zone, EMA, momentum and entry-trigger conditions are present."

Clear rules make execution easier to evaluate later.

## Your Market and Instrument

Your plan should identify what you intend to trade.

For example:

**Primary markets:**
- EURUSD
- XAUUSD

You do not need to monitor every available instrument.

Focusing on a smaller group can make it easier to understand how those markets behave and reduce unnecessary trades.

## Define Your Setup

Your trading plan must explain what qualifies as an opportunity.

For the current Dessetra framework:

**Trend â†’ Structure â†’ Zone â†’ EMA â†’ Momentum â†’ Entry Trigger**

Before entering, ask:

### Trend
Is the broader market bullish, bearish or ranging?

### Structure
Are the swings and structure supporting the direction?

### Zone
Is price reacting around a meaningful area?

### EMA
Are the 21, 50 and 200 EMA providing useful directional confirmation?

### Momentum
Does MACD support the setup rather than strongly contradict it?

### Entry Trigger
Has the actual entry condition appeared?

If important conditions are missing:

**WAIT or REJECT.**

## Define Risk Before the Session

Your plan should state your risk rules before any trade appears.

Record:

**Account Balance:**  
**Normal Risk Per Trade:**  
**Maximum Session Risk:**  
**Maximum Consecutive Losses:**  

For this course:

**Maximum combined session-risk ceiling = 5%**

And from our psychology lesson:

**Three consecutive losses = stop trading for the day**

Remember:

The 5% figure is a **maximum ceiling**, not a target.

Your normal risk may be considerably lower.

---

## Define Entry Before Execution

Do not enter simply because price is moving.

Your plan should state what must happen first.

Possible entry conditions might include:

- rejection from a planned zone
- confirmation candle
- breakout and retest
- lower-timeframe structure break
- EMA rejection with supporting context

The entry should be connected to the setup you analysed.

---

## Define the Invalidation Point

Before entering, identify:

**What would prove this trade idea wrong?**

That invalidation point helps determine the Stop Loss.

For a Buy, invalidation may be below important supporting structure.

For a Sell, it may be above important resistance or bearish structure.

Do not wait until the position is losing to decide where the trade becomes invalid.

---

## Define Stop Loss

Every planned trade should have a Stop Loss.

The Stop Loss should:

- relate to market structure
- protect capital
- define the planned loss
- be considered when calculating position size

Do not widen it simply because price is approaching it.

---

## Define Take Profit

Your target should be identified before entry.

Potential target areas may include:

- previous swing high
- previous swing low
- Support or Resistance
- Supply or Demand
- liquidity area
- another logical structural objective

Then calculate whether the resulting R:R satisfies your trading plan.

---

## Define Your R:R

Suppose:

Risk:

**20 pips**

Potential reward:

**60 pips**

R:R:

**1:3**

The Dessetra framework generally seeks approximately **1:3 when market structure allows**.

But do not force the target beyond logical structure merely to obtain the ratio.

A setup can look technically attractive and still be rejected because the available reward does not justify the planned risk.

## Pre-Trade Checklist

Before every trade, complete this checklist:

### Market Context

- [ ] I checked the higher timeframe.
- [ ] I identified the market direction.
- [ ] I identified important structure.
- [ ] Price is at or approaching a meaningful zone.

### Confirmation

- [ ] EMA conditions support the setup.
- [ ] Momentum has been checked.
- [ ] My entry trigger is present.
- [ ] I am using a completed candle where my rule requires confirmation.

### Risk

- [ ] My invalidation point is clear.
- [ ] My Stop Loss is defined.
- [ ] My monetary risk is calculated.
- [ ] My lot size is calculated.
- [ ] My Take Profit is logical.
- [ ] My R:R is acceptable.
- [ ] Total session exposure remains within my limit.

### Psychology

- [ ] I am not chasing price.
- [ ] I am not revenge trading.
- [ ] I am not entering because of FOMO.
- [ ] I am prepared to accept the planned loss.
- [ ] I can reject the trade if conditions change.

If important boxes remain unchecked:

**Do not force the trade.**

## What Is a Trading Journal?

A trading journal is a structured record of your trading decisions and results.

It helps answer questions that memory alone cannot reliably answer.

For example:

- Which setups do I trade most often?
- Do I follow my Stop Loss?
- Do I overtrade after losses?
- Which market conditions suit my approach?
- Are my losses coming from the strategy or my execution?
- Am I respecting my risk rules?

Without records, traders may repeatedly make the same mistakes without recognizing the pattern.

---

## What to Record Before a Trade

Before execution, record:

- Date
- Time
- Instrument
- Direction â€” Buy/Sell
- Higher-timeframe direction
- Market structure
- Key zone
- Entry reason
- Entry price
- Stop Loss
- Take Profit
- Stop Loss distance
- Lot size
- Risk %
- Monetary risk
- Planned R:R
- Emotional state

This captures what you believed **before knowing the result**.

That is important.

Do not rewrite your reasoning after seeing whether the trade won or lost.

---

## Screenshot Before Entry

Take a screenshot of the chart before or immediately after execution.

The screenshot should ideally show:

- market structure
- important zone
- EMA positioning
- entry
- Stop Loss
- Take Profit

This creates visual evidence of what the chart actually looked like when the decision was made.

---

## What to Record After the Trade

When the trade closes, add:

- Exit price
- Profit/Loss
- Result in R
- Exit reason
- Whether rules were followed
- Emotional behaviour during the trade
- Screenshot after completion
- What went well
- What went wrong
- Lesson learned

Do not record only winning trades.

Losing trades are equally important for reviewing execution.

---

## Measuring Results in R

Instead of looking only at dollars, you can also record results in units of risk.

If your original planned risk is:

**1R = $10**

and the trade loses the full planned amount:

**Result = -1R**

If the trade gains $20:

**Result = +2R**

If it gains $30:

**Result = +3R**

This makes trades easier to compare even when account size or position size changes.

---

## Strategy Loss vs Execution Error

Your journal should classify losing trades.

### Strategy Loss

The setup was valid.

You followed the rules.

Risk was correct.

The trade simply lost.

Record:

**Valid trade â€” normal loss**

### Execution Error

You broke your rules.

Examples:

- entered early
- chased price
- ignored confirmation
- used excessive risk
- moved the Stop Loss
- revenge traded
- entered from FOMO

Record the specific violation.

This prevents you from blaming the strategy for mistakes in execution.

---

## Journal Emotional State

Your journal should also record how you felt.

For example:

**Before trade:** Calm

**During trade:** Anxious

**After trade:** Frustrated

Over several trades, patterns may appear.

You may discover that you:

- overtrade when frustrated
- close winners early when fearful
- increase risk after winning
- chase price after missing entries

Psychological patterns become easier to correct when they are recorded.

---

## Weekly Review

After a group of trades, review the journal.

Ask:

### Execution

- How many trades followed my rules?
- How many violated them?
- Did I chase entries?
- Did I respect Stop Losses?

### Risk

- Did I calculate position size correctly?
- Did I exceed my session-risk limit?
- Did I increase risk emotionally?

### Strategy

- Which setups appeared most often?
- Which market conditions were present?
- Were losses normal strategy losses or execution errors?

### Psychology

- What emotions appeared most often?
- Did losses affect the next trade?
- Did wins create overconfidence?

The purpose is to find **patterns**, not to criticize individual trades.

---

## Do Not Change Rules After Every Loss

One losing trade does not prove a strategy is broken.

Two winning trades do not prove it is excellent.

Review performance across a meaningful sample of consistently executed trades.

If you constantly change the strategy after every result, you cannot properly evaluate whether the original rules work.

## Key Takeaways

- A trading plan defines your rules before market pressure appears.
- Your plan should define setup, entry, risk, SL, TP, R:R and stopping conditions.
- Use a pre-trade checklist before execution.
- A trading journal records what you actually did.
- Record both winning and losing trades.
- Record your reasoning before knowing the result.
- Screenshots provide visual evidence for later review.
- Track emotional state as well as financial results.
- Separate normal strategy losses from execution errors.
- Review groups of trades rather than judging the strategy from one result.
- The purpose of journaling is continuous improvement.
- Consistent execution matters more than the outcome of one trade.
