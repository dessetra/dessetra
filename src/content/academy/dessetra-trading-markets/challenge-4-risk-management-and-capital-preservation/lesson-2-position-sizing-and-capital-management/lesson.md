# Lot Size, Stop Loss & R:R

Knowing where price may move is only one part of trading.

Before entering a position, you must also know:

- how much money you are prepared to risk
- where the trade becomes invalid
- where your Stop Loss belongs
- what position size fits that risk
- where your Take Profit belongs
- whether the potential reward justifies the risk

These decisions should be made **before the trade is opened**.

The basic process is:

**Account Risk â†’ Stop Loss â†’ Position Size â†’ Take Profit â†’ Risk-to-Reward**

---

## What Is Position Size?

**Position size** is the amount of an asset or currency you control in a trade.

In Forex, position size is commonly expressed in **lots**.

Typical lot-size terminology includes:

- Standard Lot = 100,000 units
- Mini Lot = 10,000 units
- Micro Lot = 1,000 units

Many trading platforms allow smaller decimal lot sizes such as:

**0.01, 0.02, 0.10, 0.50**

A larger lot size causes each price movement to have a larger financial effect.

A smaller lot size reduces that financial effect.

Therefore:

**Lot size directly affects risk.**

---

## Do Not Choose Lot Size First

A common mistake is:

> "I normally trade 0.10 lot."

That ignores:

- account size
- Stop Loss distance
- instrument
- pip/tick value
- planned monetary risk

Instead, determine your acceptable risk first.

Then calculate the position size that fits the trade.

The correct sequence is:

**Risk â†’ Stop Loss â†’ Lot Size**

Not:

**Lot Size â†’ Hope**

---

## Step 1 â€” Determine Account Risk

Suppose your account balance is:

**$1,000**

You decide to risk:

**1%**

Your maximum planned loss is:

**$10**

Calculation:

**$1,000 Ã— 0.01 = $10**

That $10 becomes the monetary risk available for the trade.

Another example:

Account:

**$2,500**

Risk:

**2%**

Maximum planned loss:

**$50**

Calculation:

**$2,500 Ã— 0.02 = $50**

The percentage should be decided according to your risk plan before entering.

Remember the Dessetra rule:

**Total planned session risk must not exceed 5% of trading capital.**

The 5% limit is a maximum ceiling, not a target.

---

## Step 2 â€” Determine the Stop Loss

As learned in Challenge 3, a Stop Loss should not be placed at an arbitrary distance simply because you want a particular lot size.

First ask:

**Where does my trade idea become invalid?**

For a Buy setup, this might be:

- below a relevant swing low
- below Demand
- below Support
- beyond the structure supporting the bullish idea

For a Sell setup, this might be:

- above a relevant swing high
- above Supply
- above Resistance
- beyond the structure supporting the bearish idea

Your analysis determines the logical Stop Loss location.

---

## Stop Loss Distance

Once the Stop Loss location is known, measure the distance between:

**Entry â†’ Stop Loss**

Example:

Entry:

**1.1000**

Stop Loss:

**1.0980**

Difference:

**20 pips**

The trade therefore has a **20-pip Stop Loss distance**.

That distance is important because it helps determine the appropriate position size.

---

## Step 3 â€” Calculate Position Size

The general relationship is:

**Position Size = Monetary Risk Ã· Risk Per Unit of Price Movement**

For a Forex setup where pip value is being used:

**Position Size = Money Risked Ã· (Stop Loss in Pips Ã— Pip Value Per Lot)**

The important principle is:

**The wider the Stop Loss, the smaller the position should generally be for the same monetary risk.**

And:

**The tighter the Stop Loss, the larger the calculated position may be for the same monetary risk.**

But never tighten a Stop Loss artificially just to obtain a larger lot size.

**20 pips**

The position size now matches the planned monetary risk.

Keep the same:

Account:

**$1,000**

Risk:

**$10**

But now suppose the market structure requires:

**50-pip Stop Loss**

Using the same simplified EURUSD assumption:

At 0.01 lot:

**Position Size â‰ˆ 0.02 lot**

Notice what happened:

### 20-pip SL

### 50-pip SL

Same account.

Same monetary risk.

Different Stop Loss.

Different position size.

This is why your lot size should adapt to the trade.

---

## Important: Pip Value Is Not Always the Same

Do not assume that every Forex pair or instrument has the same pip or tick value.

The monetary value of price movement can depend on:

- instrument
- lot size
- account currency
- contract specifications
- current exchange rate

Gold, indices and other instruments can also use different contract and tick specifications from Forex currency pairs.

Therefore, when trading:

**Check the instrument specifications in your platform or use a reliable position-size calculator.**

The previous EURUSD example was deliberately simplified to teach the calculation principle.

---

## Stop Loss Does Not Determine How Much You Must Lose

Suppose the market structure requires a large Stop Loss.

That does **not** mean you must accept a large monetary loss.

You can reduce the position size.

For example:

Trader A:

**20-pip SL + larger lot**

Trader B:

**50-pip SL + smaller lot**

Both traders may still risk the same:

**$10**

This separates:

**market distance**

from:

**financial risk**

That distinction is fundamental to position sizing.

---

## Take Profit

After defining Entry and Stop Loss, identify the logical target.

Possible Take Profit areas include:

- previous swing high
- previous swing low
- Support
- Resistance
- Supply
- Demand
- liquidity area
- predetermined R:R objective

The target should make sense within market structure.

Do not place an unrealistic Take Profit simply to manufacture an attractive R:R.

---

## Understanding Risk-to-Reward

Risk-to-Reward compares the amount you may lose with the amount you may gain.

It is written:

**Risk : Reward**

For example:

### 1:1

Risk $10 to potentially gain $10.

### 1:2

Risk $10 to potentially gain $20.

### 1:3

Risk $10 to potentially gain $30.

If your Stop Loss distance is 20 pips:

### 1:2

Potential target distance:

**40 pips**

### 1:3

Potential target distance:

**60 pips**

---

## Dessetra R:R Framework

The original Trading Made Simple material encourages maintaining at least approximately:

**1:2 Risk-to-Reward**

Within the Dessetra course, we generally seek approximately:

**1:3 R:R**

when market structure provides enough room.

This does not mean every trade must artificially have a 1:3 target.

The market structure still matters.

---

## Do Not Force 1:3

Suppose:

Entry:

**1.1000**

Stop Loss:

**1.0980**

Risk:

**20 pips**

A 1:3 target would be approximately:

**60 pips**

But imagine major Resistance is only 25 pips above your entry.

Setting Take Profit 60 pips away simply because you want 1:3 ignores the obstacle directly ahead.

The appropriate decision may be:

**REJECT THE TRADE**

rather than manipulating the chart to fit your desired ratio.

---

## Risk-to-Reward Before Entry

R:R should be evaluated before executing the trade.

Ask:

1. Where is my Entry?
2. Where does the setup become invalid?
3. Where is my Stop Loss?
4. Where is the logical target?
5. How much am I risking?
6. How much could I reasonably gain?
7. Does the trade satisfy my plan?

If the answer is no:

**Do not enter simply because the technical setup looks attractive.**

---

## Break-Even

**Break-even** means managing the Stop Loss so that, if triggered, the trade closes around the original entry price rather than at the original planned loss.

Example:

Buy Entry:

**1.1000**

Initial Stop Loss:

**1.0980**

Price later moves favourably.

Under a predefined management rule, the Stop Loss may eventually be moved toward:

**1.1000**

If price reverses and reaches that adjusted Stop Loss, the position may close around the entry level, excluding trading costs or slippage.

---

## Do Not Move to Break-Even Randomly

Moving the Stop Loss to break-even too quickly can remove a trade during normal market movement.

The decision should follow your trading strategy.

Possible predefined conditions might include:

- price reaching a specific R:R milestone
- structure forming in your favour
- a target or management level being reached

The important point is:

**Break-even should be rule-based, not fear-based.**

---

## Never Widen the Stop to Rescue a Losing Trade

Suppose your original Stop Loss is:

**20 pips**

Price moves against you and approaches the SL.

Changing it to:

**40 pips**

simply because you do not want the loss doubles the price distance you originally planned to tolerate.

Unless the trading strategy specifically defines a valid adjustment before entry, widening the SL changes the original risk plan.

Accepting a planned loss is part of trading discipline.

**$30**

**$40**

**$90**

## Complete Position-Sizing Process

Use this sequence before entering:

### 1. Account Size
How much trading capital do I have?

### 2. Risk Percentage
What percentage am I prepared to risk?

### 3. Monetary Risk
Convert that percentage into money.

### 4. Entry
Where will the trade begin?

### 5. Invalidation
Where does my analysis become wrong?

### 6. Stop Loss
Place the SL beyond the logical invalidation area according to the setup.

### 7. Stop Distance
Measure Entry â†’ SL.

### 8. Position Size
Calculate the lot size that keeps the monetary risk within your limit.

### 9. Take Profit
Identify the logical market target.

### 10. R:R
Compare potential reward with planned risk.

### 11. Total Exposure
Check existing/open trade risk.

### 12. Decision
**EXECUTE / WAIT / REJECT**

## Key Takeaways

- Decide monetary risk before determining position size.
- The Stop Loss should come from trade invalidation and market structure.
- Lot size should adapt to account risk and Stop Loss distance.
- A wider SL generally requires a smaller position when monetary risk stays constant.
- Do not assume every instrument has the same pip or tick value.
- Take Profit should respect market structure.
- Risk-to-Reward compares planned loss with potential reward.
- Trading Made Simple uses approximately 1:2 as a minimum planning framework.
- Dessetra generally seeks approximately 1:3 when market structure supports it.
- Never force SL or TP simply to manufacture a preferred ratio.
- Break-even management should be rule-based.
- Never widen a Stop Loss simply to avoid accepting a planned loss.
- Always consider combined exposure across open trades.
- The Dessetra 5% session-risk rule remains a maximum ceiling, not a target.
