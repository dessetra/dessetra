# Building a Complete Trading Plan

A trading plan is a written set of rules that guides every important trading decision.

It tells the trader:

- what to trade
- when to trade
- when not to trade
- what setup to look for
- what confirms an entry
- how much to risk
- where to place the stop-loss
- where to place the take-profit
- how to manage open positions
- when to stop trading
- how to review performance

A complete trading plan removes as much uncertainty and emotional decision-making as possible.

Without a plan, the trader may react differently to similar market situations.

With a plan, decisions become more consistent, measurable and repeatable.

---

# What Is a Trading Plan?

A trading plan is a structured document that defines how a trader will operate in the market.

It combines:

- strategy
- risk management
- execution
- psychology
- record keeping
- performance review

A trading plan is broader than a trading strategy.

A strategy explains how a particular trade setup works.

A trading plan explains how the trader manages the entire trading process.

---

# Trading Strategy vs Trading Plan

A trading strategy may define:

- trend-following setup
- range setup
- breakout setup
- reversal setup
- entry and exit conditions

A trading plan also defines:

- markets allowed
- trading hours
- risk limits
- maximum number of trades
- session loss limits
- news restrictions
- emotional rules
- journaling process
- review schedule
- recovery procedure

The strategy is one part of the complete plan.

---

# Why a Trading Plan Is Important

A trading plan helps:

- reduce emotional decisions
- improve consistency
- control risk
- prevent overtrading
- define valid setups
- measure performance
- identify mistakes
- create accountability
- improve discipline
- separate luck from skill

The plan does not guarantee profit.

It creates a professional structure for decision-making.

---

# A Plan Must Be Written

A plan that exists only in the trader's memory can easily change under pressure.

Written rules are easier to:

- follow
- review
- test
- improve
- measure
- compare with actual results

The trading plan should be clear enough that another informed person could understand how the strategy is meant to work.

---

# Main Parts of a Complete Trading Plan

A complete plan may include:

1. Trading objectives
2. Available capital
3. Markets to trade
4. Trading sessions
5. Timeframes
6. Market conditions
7. Strategy and setup rules
8. Entry rules
9. Stop-loss rules
10. Take-profit rules

18. Drawdown response
19. Strategy improvement

Each part should be specific.

---

# Define the Trading Objective

The trader should begin by defining the purpose of trading.

Possible objectives include:

- learning professional market analysis
- developing consistency
- preserving capital
- building a long-term trading record
- creating an additional income source
- managing capital responsibly

Objectives should remain realistic.

A plan based on guaranteed returns or immediate financial freedom creates dangerous pressure.

---

# Process Goals vs Profit Goals

Profit goals focus on financial results.

Process goals focus on correct behaviour.

Examples of process goals include:

- follow every risk rule
- take only valid setups
- journal every trade
- avoid revenge trading
- stop after reaching the loss limit
- review performance weekly

Process goals are more controllable than profit goals.

A trader can follow the plan correctly even when a valid trade loses.

---

# Define the Trading Capital

The plan should state the amount of capital allocated to trading.

Trading capital should be money the trader can afford to place at risk without affecting essential needs.

It should not include money required for:

- food
- rent
- tuition
- medical care
- debt repayment
- emergency expenses
- family responsibilities

Using essential funds creates emotional pressure and poor decisions.

---

# Separate Trading Capital From Personal Money

Trading funds should be treated separately from daily personal funds.

This helps:

- measure performance accurately
- avoid emotional withdrawals
- protect essential expenses
- define clear risk percentages
- prevent repeated deposits after losses

A trader should not keep adding money to rescue uncontrolled positions.

---

# Select the Markets to Trade

The plan should define which markets are permitted.

Examples include:

- Forex
- cryptocurrencies
- stocks
- indices
- commodities
- futures

A beginner should avoid following too many markets at once.

Focusing on a smaller group can improve:

- familiarity
- analysis quality
- execution speed
- record keeping
- consistency

---

# Define the Instruments

The trader may list specific instruments.

Example:

Forex:

- EUR/USD
- GBP/USD
- USD/JPY

Crypto:

- BTC/USDT
- ETH/USDT
- BNB/USDT

The purpose is to avoid random trading across unfamiliar assets.

---

# Understand Each Market

Before adding an instrument to the plan, understand:

- trading hours
- volatility
- spread
- liquidity
- contract size
- leverage rules
- fees
- major news drivers
- tick or pip value
- order types

Different markets require different risk calculations.

---

# Define Trading Sessions

The plan should state when trading is allowed.

Examples include:

- London session
- New York session
- London and New York overlap
- selected crypto hours
- specific market-opening periods

The trader should also define when trading is prohibited.

---

# Session Rules

Possible session rules include:

- trade only during selected hours
- do not open new trades near session close
- avoid low-liquidity periods
- stop after a fixed number of trades
- stop after reaching the session loss limit
- do not trade when tired or distracted

Clear session rules reduce random activity.

---

# Define the Timeframes

The trading plan should specify which timeframes are used for:

- higher-timeframe direction
- setup identification
- entry confirmation
- trade management

Example:

- Daily chart for broad direction
- Four-hour chart for important zones
- One-hour chart for setup
- Fifteen-minute chart for entry confirmation

This structure should remain consistent.

---

# Avoid Random Timeframe Switching

A trader may switch timeframes emotionally to justify a desired trade.

Examples include:

- moving to a lower timeframe to find confirmation
- moving to a higher timeframe to ignore invalidation
- searching until one chart supports the desired direction

The plan should define the role of each timeframe before trading begins.

---

# Define Acceptable Market Conditions

The plan should state the market conditions in which the strategy is allowed.

Examples include:

- strong uptrend
- strong downtrend
- confirmed horizontal range
- breakout from consolidation
- pullback into support or resistance
- high-liquidity session

The strategy should not be used in every condition.

---

# Define Conditions to Avoid

Possible conditions to avoid include:

- unclear market structure
- extreme spread
- very low liquidity
- major news approaching
- overextended price
- unstable platform conditions
- emotional distress
- severe account drawdown
- excessive correlation across open positions

Avoidance rules are just as important as entry rules.

---

# Choose the Trading Strategies

The trader should clearly list the strategies permitted in the plan.

Example:

1. Trend pullback strategy
2. Breakout and retest strategy
3. Range support and resistance strategy

Each strategy should have its own rules.

A trader should not create a new strategy while a trade is forming.

---

# Strategy Name

Give each strategy a simple name.

Examples:

- Bullish Trend Pullback
- Bearish Breakout Retest
- Range Support Reversal
- Range Resistance Reversal

This makes journaling and performance review easier.

---

# Setup Conditions

The setup section should define what must exist before a trade can be considered.

Example bullish trend pullback setup:

- higher timeframe is bullish
- price forms Higher Highs and Higher Lows
- price pulls back into support
- major bearish news is not approaching
- enough space exists to the next resistance
- risk-to-reward is acceptable

If a required condition is missing, the setup is incomplete.

---

# Entry Confirmation Rules

The plan should define the exact confirmation required.

Possible confirmation includes:

- Bullish Engulfing candle
- Bearish Engulfing candle
- rejection wick
- Break of Structure
- breakout close
- successful retest
- momentum candle
- volume expansion

The trader should not use vague language such as:

"Enter when the market looks good."

Rules must be measurable.

---

# Entry Trigger

The entry trigger is the final condition that activates the trade.

Example:

- enter after the confirmation candle closes
- enter after price breaks the confirmation candle high
- enter after the breakout level is successfully retested
- place a pending order at a predefined level

The trigger should be known before the trade develops.

---

# Order Type

The plan should define which order type will be used.

Possible choices include:

- market order
- limit order
- stop order
- stop-limit order

The order type should match the strategy.

Example:

A pullback strategy may use a limit order.

A breakout strategy may use a stop order or market order after confirmation.

---

# Stop-Loss Rules

The trading plan should define how the stop-loss is placed.

Possible rules include:

- below the Higher Low for a bullish trade
- above the Lower High for a bearish trade
- outside the support or resistance zone
- beyond the breakout retest structure
- beyond the setup invalidation point
- adjusted for normal volatility

The stop-loss should not be chosen from the amount of money the trader wants to lose.

It should be based on market structure.

---

# Stop-Loss Is Mandatory

Every trade should have a predefined risk-control method.

For most retail traders, this means placing a stop-loss when the trade is opened.

The trader should not depend only on manually closing a losing trade.

Technical failure, emotional hesitation or rapid market movement may prevent timely action.

---

# Stop Movement Rules

The plan should state whether the stop may be moved.

Possible rules include:

- never widen the stop
- move to break-even only after a defined structure forms
- trail below new Higher Lows
- trail above new Lower Highs
- adjust only after partial profit is taken

The stop should never be moved farther away simply to avoid accepting a loss.

---

# Take-Profit Rules

The target may be based on:

- next support
- next resistance
- previous swing high or low
- measured pattern target
- fixed risk-to-reward
- trend structure
- partial profit levels

The plan should define how targets are selected.

---

# Risk-to-Reward Standard

For this course, traders should generally aim for an average risk-to-reward ratio of approximately:

**1:3**

This means risking one unit to potentially gain three units.

However, market structure must realistically support the target.

A trader should not place a target beyond major obstacles simply to force a 1:3 ratio.

---

# Minimum Acceptable Risk-to-Reward

The plan may define a minimum ratio.

Example:

- preferred ratio: 1:3
- minimum acceptable ratio: 1:2
- reject setups below 1:2

This is only an example.

The minimum should be tested against the strategy's win rate and trading costs.

---

# Position Sizing Rules

Position size must be calculated before every trade.

It should be based on:

- account balance
- chosen risk percentage
- stop-loss distance
- pip, point or tick value
- contract size

The position size should not be selected based on emotion or expected profit.

---

# Risk Per Trade

The plan should define the maximum risk per trade.

Example:

- normal risk: 1%
- reduced risk during drawdown: 0.5%
- no trade may exceed 1%

The exact number depends on the trader's tested strategy and tolerance.

Lower risk can help reduce drawdown.

---

# Maximum Session Risk

For this course:

**Total risk during one trading session must never exceed 5% of total trading capital.**

This includes:

- all open positions
- pending orders that may activate
- correlated trades
- additional trades under consideration

The 5% level is a maximum ceiling, not a target.

Many professional plans may use a lower limit.

---

# Daily Loss Limit

The plan may define a maximum daily loss.

Example:

- stop after losing 3% in one day
- stop after three consecutive losses
- stop after two rule violations

The exact rule should be written before the session begins.

---

# Weekly Loss Limit

A weekly loss limit can prevent one difficult period from becoming a major drawdown.

Example:

- stop for the week after a 6% decline
- review all trades
- resume only after completing the review

This protects both capital and emotional stability.

---

# Monthly Drawdown Limit

A monthly drawdown limit may trigger a deeper review.

Example:

- reduce risk after 5% drawdown
- pause live trading after 10% drawdown
- return to demo trading after 15% drawdown

These percentages are examples.

The actual limits should reflect the trader's strategy and risk tolerance.

---

# Maximum Number of Trades

The plan may limit the number of trades per session or day.

This can reduce:

- overtrading
- revenge trading
- boredom trading
- transaction costs
- low-quality setups

Example:

Maximum of three trades per session.

---

# Consecutive Loss Rule

A trader may stop after a defined losing streak.

Example:

After three consecutive losses:

- stop trading
- review the setups
- calculate session risk
- check emotional state
- continue only during a later session if rules allow

This rule prevents emotional escalation.

---

# Correlation Rules

The plan should define how correlated positions are handled.

Examples:

- do not risk 1% on several trades driven by the same currency
- treat highly correlated crypto positions as one combined exposure
- reduce position size when trades depend on the same market event

Several positions can create one large hidden risk.

---

# Leverage Rules

The plan should state how leverage is used.

Possible rules include:

- use only the leverage necessary for the calculated position
- never increase leverage to recover losses
- avoid maximum broker leverage
- reduce exposure during high volatility
- confirm liquidation distance for leveraged products

Leverage should support position sizing, not replace risk control.

---

# News Rules

The trader should define how major news affects trading.

Possible rules include:

- no new trade within 30 minutes of high-impact news
- close short-term positions before major announcements
- hold only if the strategy specifically allows it
- check the economic calendar before every session

The exact rule depends on the strategy.

---

# Weekend Rules

For markets that close over the weekend, the plan may define:

- whether trades may remain open
- how gap risk is handled
- whether position size is reduced
- which news events must be checked

For cryptocurrencies, weekend trading may have different liquidity conditions.

---

# Trade Management Rules

The plan should explain what happens after entry.

Possible management styles include:

- fixed stop and fixed target
- partial profit
- break-even adjustment
- trailing stop
- structure-based exit
- time-based exit

The trader should not change management style randomly during a trade.

---

# Fixed Stop and Target

This method leaves the original stop-loss and take-profit unchanged.

Possible advantage:

- simple
- consistent
- easy to test
- less emotional interference

Possible disadvantage:

- does not adapt to changing structure

It may be suitable for traders who struggle with overmanagement.

---

# Break-Even Rule

A break-even rule should specify when the stop may move to the entry price.

Example:

Move to break-even only after:

- price reaches 1:1
- a new structure forms
- the first target is reached
- momentum remains favourable

Moving too early may close a valid trade during normal movement.

---

# Partial Profit Rule

The plan should specify:

- percentage of position to close
- level where partial profit is taken
- what happens to the stop-loss
- final target for the remaining position

Example:

- close 50% at 1:2
- move stop according to structure
- hold 50% toward 1:3

This changes the average reward and should be tested.

---

# Trailing Stop Rule

The plan may trail the stop using:

- new Higher Lows
- new Lower Highs
- moving average
- fixed distance
- volatility measure
- trendline

The method should be consistent.

---

# Time-Based Exit

A time-based exit closes a trade after a defined period if expected movement does not occur.

Example:

- close at session end
- close after five candles without continuation
- cancel pending order after two hours

This may help avoid inactive or stale setups.

---

# Early Exit Rules

The plan may allow an early exit when:

- market structure invalidates
- price strongly rejects the setup
- major unexpected news occurs
- liquidity disappears
- platform conditions become unsafe

Early exits should follow rules rather than fear.

---

# Pending Order Rules

The plan should state:

- when pending orders may be used
- how long they remain active
- when they expire
- whether stop-loss and target are attached
- what invalidates them before activation

Old orders should not remain active after the setup changes.

---

# Trade Cancellation Rules

A trade setup should be cancelled when:

- confirmation fails
- price reaches the target before entry
- stop distance becomes too wide
- risk-to-reward becomes unacceptable
- session limit is reached
- news conditions change
- market structure changes
- the planned trading window closes

Not entering is part of disciplined trading.

---

# Psychological Rules

A complete plan should include emotional and behavioural rules.

Examples:

- do not trade while angry
- do not trade after severe stress
- do not chase missed trades
- do not increase risk after a loss
- do not increase risk after a winning streak
- stop after breaking two rules
- take a break after a major emotional reaction

Psychological discipline should be treated as part of risk management.

---

# Revenge Trading Rule

A possible rule is:

After a stopped-out trade:

1. Record the result.
2. Leave the chart for a defined period.
3. Reanalyse the market.
4. Enter again only if a completely new valid setup forms.
5. Do not increase position size.

This reduces revenge trading.

---

# Fear of Missing Out Rule

A possible rule is:

If price has moved beyond the planned entry and the original risk-to-reward is no longer available, cancel the trade.

Do not chase.

Wait for a new setup.

---

# Boredom Trading Rule

A trader should not enter simply because no trades have appeared.

Possible rule:

If no valid setup appears during the session, record "No Trade" and close the platform.

A no-trade day can be a successful day.

---

# Confidence and Overconfidence

After several wins, the trader should continue using the same risk rules.

Winning does not justify:

- larger random positions
- weaker setups
- more trades
- excessive leverage
- ignoring confirmation

The plan must remain stable during winning periods.

---

# Trading Journal

The plan should require every trade to be recorded.

The journal may include:

- date
- market
- session
- strategy name
- market condition
- higher-timeframe direction
- entry trigger
- entry price
- stop-loss
- target
- position size
- risk percentage
- result
- screenshot
- emotional state
- rule violations
- lesson learned

The journal provides evidence for improvement.

---

# Before-and-After Screenshots

Screenshots can show:

- original analysis
- planned entry
- market structure
- stop-loss
- target
- final result

A screenshot before entry helps reveal whether the trade was genuinely planned.

A screenshot after exit helps evaluate management.

---

# Process Score

The trader may score each trade based on rule-following.

Example:

| Area | Score |
|---|---:|
| Correct market condition | 1 |
| Valid setup | 1 |
| Confirmation present | 1 |
| Correct position size | 1 |
| Stop-loss respected | 1 |
| Target rule followed | 1 |
| No emotional interference | 1 |

A high process score is valuable even when the trade loses.

---

# Daily Review

At the end of the session, review:

- number of trades
- total risk
- total result
- valid setups
- rule violations
- emotional behaviour
- execution quality
- lessons learned

The daily review should be brief but consistent.

---

# Weekly Review

A weekly review may examine:

- total trades
- win rate
- average win
- average loss
- risk-to-reward
- expectancy
- best-performing strategy
- worst-performing strategy
- largest drawdown
- repeated mistakes
- session performance
- market performance

This helps identify patterns.

---

# Monthly Review

A monthly review may evaluate:

- total return
- maximum drawdown
- strategy consistency
- risk compliance
- emotional discipline
- missed opportunities
- execution errors
- necessary improvements

Monthly review should focus on both financial results and process quality.

---

# Strategy Performance Metrics

Useful performance metrics include:

- number of trades
- win rate
- loss rate
- average winning trade
- average losing trade
- profit factor
- expectancy
- maximum drawdown
- longest losing streak
- average risk-to-reward
- rule compliance rate

One metric should not be judged alone.

---

# Win Rate

Win rate is the percentage of trades that are profitable.

Formula:

**Win Rate = Winning Trades ÷ Total Trades × 100**

A high win rate does not always mean a profitable strategy.

The size of wins and losses also matters.

---

# Average Win and Average Loss

Average win is the average profit from winning trades.

Average loss is the average loss from losing trades.

A strategy can remain profitable with a lower win rate if its average win is significantly larger than its average loss.

---

# Expectancy

Expectancy estimates the average result expected per trade.

Formula:

**Expectancy = (Win Rate × Average Win) - (Loss Rate × Average Loss)**

Example:

Win rate:

40%

Average win:

3R

Loss rate:

60%

Average loss:

1R

Calculation:

(0.40 × 3R) - (0.60 × 1R)

1.2R - 0.6R = 0.6R

The strategy has positive expectancy in this simplified example.

---

# Profit Factor

Profit factor compares total gross profit with total gross loss.

Formula:

**Profit Factor = Total Gross Profit ÷ Total Gross Loss**

A value above 1 means gross profit exceeded gross loss.

The result should be examined over a meaningful sample.

---

# Meaningful Sample Size

A strategy should not be changed after only a few trades.

A small sample may be affected by luck.

The trader may review:

- 20 trades
- 50 trades
- 100 trades
- different market conditions

The required sample depends on trade frequency and strategy type.

---

# Backtesting

Backtesting applies strategy rules to historical market data.

It helps estimate:

- win rate
- average reward
- drawdown
- losing streaks
- market suitability
- rule clarity

Backtesting does not guarantee future results.

Historical conditions may differ from future conditions.

---

# Forward Testing

Forward testing applies the strategy to live or simulated current markets.

It may be done through:

- demo account
- paper trading
- very small live risk

Forward testing helps evaluate:

- real-time decision-making
- execution
- spread
- slippage
- emotional response
- strategy practicality

---

# Demo Trading

A demo account can help traders:

- learn the platform
- test order types
- practise the plan
- verify position sizing
- collect strategy data
- improve execution

Demo trading should still follow realistic risk rules.

Random demo trades provide weak evidence.

---

# Moving From Demo to Live Trading

A trader should consider live trading only after showing:

- clear strategy rules
- consistent execution
- reliable journaling
- controlled drawdown
- positive or improving expectancy
- respect for risk limits

Initial live risk should remain small.

---

# Drawdown Response Plan

The trading plan should define what happens during drawdown.

Example:

### 0% to 5% Drawdown

- continue normal risk
- review execution

### 5% to 10% Drawdown

- reduce risk
- review recent trades
- remove weak setups

### 10% to 15% Drawdown

- pause live trading
- conduct full review
- return to demo testing

### Above 15% Drawdown

- stop trading
- investigate strategy and execution
- resume only after evidence supports it

These thresholds are examples.

---

# Recovery Rules

A recovery plan may require:

- no increased risk
- reduced position size
- only highest-quality setups
- complete journal review
- demo practice
- gradual return to normal risk

Recovery should focus on restoring discipline, not recovering money quickly.

---

# Changing the Trading Plan

The plan may be improved when evidence shows that a rule is ineffective.

Changes should not be made:

- during an open trade
- immediately after one loss
- because of frustration
- because another trader uses different rules
- to justify a desired entry

Changes should be based on data.

---

# Version Control

The trader may keep versions of the plan.

Example:

- Trading Plan Version 1.0
- Trading Plan Version 1.1
- Trading Plan Version 2.0

Each change should record:

- date
- rule changed
- reason
- supporting evidence
- expected effect

This prevents random strategy drift.

---

# One Change at a Time

Changing several rules at once makes it difficult to know which change affected performance.

A more disciplined process is:

1. Identify one problem.
2. Propose one adjustment.
3. Test the adjustment.
4. Compare results.
5. Keep or reject the change.

# Sample Complete Trading Plan

## Trading Objective

Build consistency while protecting capital and following a rule-based process.

## Markets

- EUR/USD
- GBP/USD
- BTC/USDT

## Trading Sessions

- London session
- New York session
- no new trades outside defined hours

## Timeframes

- Daily for direction
- Four-hour for structure
- One-hour for setup
- Fifteen-minute for confirmation

## Strategies

- trend pullback
- breakout and retest
- range boundary reversal

## Entry Rule

Enter only after the complete setup and confirmation trigger appear.

## Stop-Loss Rule

Place the stop beyond the structural invalidation point.

Never widen the stop.

## Take-Profit Rule

Target the next meaningful level or an approximately 1:3 risk-to-reward objective when realistic.

## Risk Per Trade

Maximum 1% per trade.

## Session Risk

Maximum total session risk of 5%.

## Daily Stop Rule

Stop after three losses or after reaching the daily loss limit.

## News Rule

No new trade shortly before high-impact news.

## Trade Management

Use the predefined stop and target.

Move the stop only according to tested structure rules.

## Journal Rule

Record every trade with screenshots and emotional notes.

## Review Rule

Review daily, weekly and monthly.

## Drawdown Rule

Reduce risk after a defined drawdown and pause live trading after the maximum threshold.

This example should be adapted and tested.

# Pre-Session Checklist

Before trading:

1. Is the platform working correctly?
2. Is the internet connection stable?
3. Have major news events been checked?
4. Which markets are allowed today?
5. What is the higher-timeframe direction?
6. What key levels are marked?
7. What strategies are permitted?
8. What is the maximum session risk?
9. How much risk is already open?
10. Am I mentally and physically prepared?
11. What conditions will make me stop trading?

---

# Pre-Trade Checklist

Before entering:

1. Is the market condition suitable?
2. Is this one of my approved strategies?
3. Is the setup complete?
4. Has confirmation appeared?
5. What is the entry trigger?
6. Where is the invalidation point?
7. Where is the stop-loss?
8. Where is the target?
9. Is risk-to-reward acceptable?
10. Is position size correct?
11. Is total session risk below 5%?
12. Are open positions correlated?
13. Is spread acceptable?
14. Is major news approaching?
15. What cancels the trade?

---

# Post-Trade Checklist

After the trade:

1. Was the setup valid?
2. Was confirmation present?
3. Was position size correct?
4. Was the stop respected?
5. Was the target rule followed?
6. Was there emotional interference?
7. Was the result recorded?
8. Were screenshots saved?
9. What lesson was learned?
10. Does the plan need review after a larger sample?

# Common Beginner Mistakes

Avoid these mistakes:

- trading without a written plan
- creating rules during an open trade
- using too many strategies
- trading too many markets
- switching timeframes emotionally
- entering without confirmation
- placing random stop-losses
- using fixed large position sizes
- changing risk after wins or losses
- ignoring correlated exposure
- forcing daily profit targets
- trading during prohibited news periods
- leaving pending orders active
- moving stops farther away
- failing to journal trades
- changing the strategy after a few losses
- exceeding the 5% session risk limit

---

# Key Principle

A trading plan turns trading from a series of emotional decisions into a structured process.

The plan should define what to do before, during and after every trade.

- position sizing
- risk limits
- trade management
